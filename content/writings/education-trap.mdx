I was eighteen when I figured this out.

Ignore that while you read this. The dates and the documents don't care how old I am.

## What we already know

Around [12.97 million](https://educationforallinindia.com/computation-of-out-of-school-children-based-on-udiseplus-2021-22/) young people in India were out of school in 2021-22, aged six to seventeen.

Not behind. Out. Many of them can't read or write.

We've all known this since we were sitting in a classroom ourselves.

It's been background noise our whole lives.

But the part nobody asked me to look at was the other side of it — the kids who *are* in school.

Including me.

## Somebody built this on purpose

Before anything else, go and find out who invented the school system. When. And why.

Because it was invented. It didn't grow out of the ground.

India didn't always do this. There was no culture here of sitting in rows, memorising a syllabus, and turning up for an exam.

Read the [Vedas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas) and you find [gurukuls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gurukula). Education in a home, built around the Vedas, philosophy and the arts. That ran from roughly 1500 BCE to 500 BCE.

By the [Maurya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurya_Empire) and [Gupta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gupta_Empire) empires, we had [Nalanda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nalanda_mahavihara) and [Taxila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxila). Students travelled across regions to reach them.

So where did all of that go?

## Prussia

The [Prussian education system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussian_education_system) was admired for one thing: efficiency.

It produced a disciplined, literate, productive workforce, exactly matched to a country that was industrialising.

That was the goal. Not curiosity. Not judgement. A literate citizenry that could meet the administrative and industrial needs of the state.

Basic literacy, basic maths, vocational skills. Enough to be productive in a job market. Not a drop more.

Britain watched this and copied it.

The Industrial Revolution was pulling people off farms and into factories, and those people needed to read instructions, operate machines and keep accounts.

The [Elementary Education Act of 1870](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_Education_Act_1870) — the Forster Act — laid the foundation for compulsory schooling and government oversight.

Standardised education, straight from the Prussian playbook.

## Then it arrived here

The British brought that same model to India between 1858 and 1947. Western structure, English language, modern subjects.

Traditional systems declined. That wasn't a side effect.

The [English Education Act of 1835](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Education_Act_1835) is the document to read.

After it, English-medium schools and colleges spread — science, medicine, law, literature, all modelled on British institutions.

The curriculum was designed to serve the colonial administration's needs. Not the development of Indian society.

It devalued what was already here, and it produced a new class of English-educated Indians who kept the British Empire running.

That policy has a name. [Macaulayism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macaulayism), after the man who argued for it.

That's what the system was for. It worked.

## Nobody turned it off

Then we got independence, and the government needed employees.

The machine for producing employees was already installed and running. So we kept it.

We still sort colleges into Tier 1, Tier 2 and Tier 3. We still run [education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_in_India) at the state level. The shape has barely moved.

Which means the trap the British built to staff an empire is now the path you're on.

## "You need a degree"

I've heard this my whole life. Skip school and you'll not get a job.

People mean it kindly. They're also right, in a narrow way.

Yes — a degree is all you need. If you want to be a job seeker. If you want to be an employee. If you're willing to put your own ideas down. And if you're ready to hand the same advice to your kid.

The degree certificate wasn't invented to prove you can think. It was invented so the government could hire factory workers quickly.

Look at how many government jobs are open now. Then look at where everyone ends up anyway.

If you're genuinely good at something, you'll get work without the paper.

## Skills over degrees

Here's the real cost of the factory.

Most of us come out of it with no idea what we're good at, or what we care about.

We were never given a chance to touch the real world and find out.

So go and look. Watch what people are building. Notice what you can't stop thinking about.

The thing that could get you where you want to go — that's your skill. Find it, then keep sharpening it.

No degree can stop you. And no degree can substitute for it.

Getting out of the race is your job. Nobody is going to do it for you.
